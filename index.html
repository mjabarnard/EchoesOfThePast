Echoes of the Past

Scarborough's Lost Aquarium

What happens when a building that holds meaning for a community is demolished? Is it gone without a trace, or does it leave echoes for future generations? Inspired by the history of the Scarborough Aquarium/People's Palace/Gala Land, we have attempted to evoke a sense of place using history and sound.

Using three-dimensional recording technology we have captured the scale of the existing car park and of the grand Victorian arcade that still exists at the Brighton SEA LIFE Centre. Combining these, we have created a soundscape that allows us to tell the story of Scarborough's Aquarium as if heard in the building itself.

Entrance Court

Scarborough Aquarium was opened in 1877 and was designed by architect Eugenius Birch. Birch designed famous piers all round Britain such as those at Brighton, Blackpool, and Eastbourne. He also designed Scarborough's North Pier, which was destroyed by storms in 1905.

Central Arcade

New technology in the Victorian age allowed for sea creatures to be kept alive in tanks that mimicked the ecosystem of the sea. This led to a worldwide craze for aquariums, such as this one in Boston, Massachusetts. Scarborough's tanks sat among spectacular Indian-inspired arches designed by Eugenius Birch.

The Stage

Set against rock walls decorated with ferns, the Aquarium included a stage for entertainments, hosting many of the top music-hall acts of the day including musicians, jugglers, and even performing cats. All-female orchestras were particularly popular.

Underwater Feats

Captain Matthew Webb, famous for being the first person to swim the English Channel, put on a seventy-four-hour swimming display in the largest tank at Scarborough Aquarium in 1880. A contemporary sketch shows a similar feat performed at Westminster Aquarium in the same year.

The Grotto

The original grotto was a cavernous space with picturesque rock walls and trickling waterfalls. This was the site of a dramatic incident in 1925, when a tram slipped backwards and crashed thirty feet through the glass ceiling.
